The cheap office chair is paid off in seat hours

A work-from-home budget already prices the desk, the monitor, sometimes even the keyboard, and the chair gets whatever is left. The usual comparison treats price as the number. For a chair that carries a working day, the honest unit is cost per seated hour, and the gap between two chairs is mostly an argument about how long the seat lasts. Take a 40 hour week, 48 working weeks, and 1,920 hours a year at the desk.

Set A costs $150 and lasts about two years in typical service before the seat pan flattens and the arms crack. That is 3,840 hours of service, $0.039 per hour. Set B costs $650 and carries a 12 year warranty, 23,040 hours, which is $0.028 per hour. The expensive chair is cheaper every hour you sit in it. Set A also loses its adjustability before it loses the seat, so hours 800 through 3,840 are billed at full price for a chair that no longer fits.

Gas lift cylinders and casters are the parts that actually fail, and they are the first place to look when reading a warranty. A replacement cylinder for the cheap chair runs $25 to $40 plus shipping, which on a $150 chair is as much as a quarter of the purchase again, and the cylinder is the one part the warranty usually excludes. When the price of one spare part approaches the price of the whole product, the manufacturer has told you what the warranty is worth.

There is also a body bill that never reaches the receipt. A seat that flattens shifts the rider into a slouch, and physical therapy copays start at $30 a visit, so a six visit run is $180, more than the first chair cost. The $500 spread between the two chairs is less than three of those runs.

The chair is billed by the hour, whether you price it that way or not.
